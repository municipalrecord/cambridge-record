Cambridge Pubic Health Department Artificial Turf Risk Assessment February 25, 2015 Summary A limited-scope risk assessment conducted by the Cambridge Public Health Department (CPHD) found no unusual risk of adverse health effects from exposures related to artificial turf. The purpose of the risk assessment was to evaluate the fields at Danehy Park used by older children and consider how these children may come into contact with chemical constituents related to the field in the air over the playing fields. Hence, this assessment did not evaluate potential exposures to very young children (e.g., toddlers) that may engage in hand-to-mouth activity that would result in incidental ingestion of crumb rubber particles from the fields. The technical details, including discussion of risk, study methods, conclusions, and limitations follow. To put this risk assessment into context, the first three sections below discuss the general nature of risk assessment and what questions the process can answer. Section 4 provides the scientific and technical details of the limited-scope risk assessment conducted by the Cambridge Public Health Department, and section 5 discusses the conclusions and limitations of this assessment. Tables with calculations and an Appendix with references follow.

2 1. What is risk and what can a risk assessment accomplish? In an environmental risk assessment, risk is usually defined as the likelihood that exposure to a hazard in the environment will lead to an adverse health impact. Risk is only possible when both a hazard is present and an exposure to that hazard occurs (i.e., hazard + exposure = risk). For example, a typical household cleaning product, such as bleach, may be a hazard. However, risk of harm would only exist if one is exposed to the bleach (i.e., it enters the body). Bleach could enter the body by a child opening the bottle and swallowing it. Bleach could also enter the body of an adult inhaling the vapors while working with it in a closed room with no ventilation. In this example, just a closed bottle of bleach sitting in a cabinet would not result in harm. However, inhaling bleach vapors or drinking the contents may result in harm. Once a substance enters the body, the degree to which harm could occur depends on how much enters the body and how often that substance is taken into the body over time (i.e., frequency of exposure). The concept of how much enters the body is often referred to as the “dose.” How frequently and how often a substance is taken into the body in repeat exposures is referred to as the “exposure duration” and is reflected in the calculation of dose. Drinking and inhaling vapors can be defined as “routes of exposure” (i.e., the means by which the hazard enters the body). In a formal risk assessment, risk is quantified as the probability of an adverse health outcome given exposure to a hazard. Risk assessments are used by environmental, public health, and other regulatory agencies to guide decision-making when evaluating complex and multi-faceted issues. In short, environmental risk assessments provide information on how likely it would be that a population exposed to a certain hazard could be harmed by that exposure. If harm is likely, then decisions can be made to take steps to prevent such a situation from occurring. 2. Understanding risk and making decisions about risk People routinely encounter and accept some level of risk in their daily activities. For example, getting an X-ray or CT scan exposes a person to low levels of radiation (i.e., some low level of risk). However, an individual and their physician weigh this risk against the benefit of doing such a procedure and make a decision. In another example, driving a car is something many people do every day. There is some risk of a car accident when one is driving, but people choose to drive because the benefit outweighs the risk. Conversely, one may choose not to drive in icy or hazardous conditions because in that case the risk outweighs the benefit. In reality, there really is no situation in daily life that is completely absent of some level of risk, even remote (i.e., there is no “zero” level of risk). Individuals make decisions to
Page 3 of 16
3 do one activity or another based on what risks they may encounter. Because there is no “zero” level of risk in any activity, one can define a level of risk at which point it no longer makes sense to engage in a certain activity because the risk outweighs the benefit, such as the driving example above. In risk assessment terms, this level at which the risk outweighs the benefit is typically referred to as an “unacceptable” level of risk. In particular, it is standard practice in environmental risk assessment to set a single level or bracket a range of levels at which exposure to environmental hazards results in an “unacceptable” risk of adverse health effects. In this context, risk is usually expressed in relation to the background population rates of adverse health outcomes (i.e., baseline incidence of disease in the population). In the case of cancer effects, the lifetime risk of more than 1 person in a population of 1 million people diagnosed with cancer above the baseline rate of cancer incidence in the population is considered “unacceptable” risk by most regulatory agencies. This probability is often expressed as a cancer risk greater than 1/1,000,000 or cancer risk > 1x10-6. Many chemicals exert toxic effects resulting in non-cancer health endpoints (e.g., kidney disease, decreased liver function, neurological effects). Non-cancer health endpoints are assessed differently than cancer endpoints. As referenced above, the “dose” is the amount of a substance that has entered the human body and may result in an adverse health impact. In considering non-cancer risk, the “dose” is compared to a concentration derived from toxicity tests that is known to be without adverse health effect. In other words, non-cancer risk is expressed as a ratio which may be greater than or less than 1. This ratio is called a “hazard quotient.” A non-cancer hazard quotient greater than 1 would be considered an “unacceptable” risk because the dose is greater than the level below which we would not expect harm. Similarly, a non-cancer hazard quotient less than 1 would not be expected to result in adverse health effects because the dose is lower than the level below which we would not expect harm. Some chemicals could have both cancer and non-cancer health endpoints, usually at different levels or frequency of exposure. Therefore, a typical risk assessment considers both carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic health endpoints. Because cancer is a disease that is multi-factorial and develops over many years, cancer risk is evaluated over a lifetime (EPA recommends using 78 years for lifetime risk calculations). Conversely, non-cancer risk is evaluated over the duration of the exposure. 3. General Risk Assessment Methodology Standard risk assessment methodology, as prescribed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, is typically used for assessing the risk of adverse human health effects from exposure to chemical and physical contaminants in the environment (EPA, 1989). Various public health agencies assess potential health impact from
Page 4 of 16
4 environmental exposures by using standard EPA risk assessment methodologies. Sometimes modified methodologies are used that are more specifically adapted to public health assessments, such as those methods prescribed by the CDC Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR). Regardless of the chosen method, every risk assessment includes the following 4 steps: • Data Evaluation and Hazard Identification • Dose-Response Assessment • Exposure Assessment • Risk Characterization In general, environmental data are gathered in the data evaluation and hazard identification step (i.e., step one). These data are separated by environmental media (e.g., air, water, soil, etc.) and are screened to narrow down the focus of the risk assessment to those chemicals that could potentially contribute to adverse health effects. If all chemicals are screened out at step 1 then the risk assessment is complete. If chemicals remain after screening, only those compounds that remain are carried forward through the next steps (i.e., steps 2-4) of the risk assessment. The second step, dose-response assessment, is the process of determining how much of a toxic compound is required to increase the likelihood of specific health effects (e.g., cancer, non-cancer) occurring above the normal background rate (in the absence of exposure to the compound). This step involves examining toxicity studies in the literature, weighing all of the evidence of a compound’s potential to cause the health effect of concern, and estimating how much of that compound must make its way into the human body to cause that particular health effect. EPA has references for toxicity factors which are used in making this determination. The third step, exposure assessment, examines the way in which humans could be exposed to a hazard (i.e., how a hazardous substance could enter the body) and what populations of people may be exposed (e.g., adults, children). Using exposure to artificial turf as an example, players on the field could inhale dust and vapors from the rubber granules that make up the “infill” of the field, or they could ingest these small rubber granules. Hence, inhalation and ingestion are the possible “routes” of exposure. The exposure assessment is used to build a scenario of who may be the most susceptible population to exposure. Typically, conservative assumptions are made such that further calculations are protective of the most susceptible populations. For example, both children and adults may use a field for recreation, but children are most likely the population of concern because they ingest and inhale more per kilogram (kg) body weight than adults. After the exposure assessment step, the last step of the risk assessment is to put all the information together and quantify the risk of adverse effect from exposure to a hazard.

5 Risk characterization is the final step of a risk assessment. Risk characterization involves a calculation of a “dose,” meaning a reflection of the amount of a compound that could actually make its way into the human body and may be associated with an adverse health outcome. The concepts of dose and frequency of exposure are critical to understanding risk. For example, one may take a single painkiller to soothe a headache without any negative health impact. Or, one may even take a single painkiller each day for many months without adverse health impact. However, if one were to take the whole bottle of painkillers in one sitting, the dose could be life-threatening. Assumptions are made in the risk characterization step to consider the range of possibilities for how much, how long, and how often a person may be exposed to a particular compound and then use this information to predict the likelihood (or probability) of an adverse health effect. Data from the dose-response assessment step and assumptions made during the exposure assessment steps are relied on for the risk characterization. Evaluating a range of assumptions in the exposure assessment and carrying that information forward in the risk characterization gives broad information on the likelihood of health impact in a number of different exposure scenarios. Evaluating a worst case scenario (i.e., using the most conservative assumptions) overestimates risk predictions such that if this scenario suggests no unacceptable risk, decision-makers can be reasonably assured that no adverse health outcomes would be expected. 4. Cambridge Public Health Department Risk Assessment Data Evaluation and Hazard Indentification For this risk assessment, the data evaluation and hazard identification steps included a full literature review to gather the necessary data for consideration. A scientific peer- reviewed literature search was conducted via PubMed with various combinations of search terms, including but not limited to “artificial turf” and “exposure” and “health effects.” The results were combined with a comprehensive search for literature produced by various government agencies, including local, state, national and international jurisdictions. Some of these references were also cross-referenced on PubMed. Finally, independent reports produced by private organizations, including industry publications and non-profit public health organizations were located. A partial summary of the literature review is included in Appendix A. The compiled literature was reviewed for data that would be applicable to the risk assessment. Specifically, data were gathered and summarized from the various studies that measured levels of chemicals detected in the outdoor air in relation to the artificial turf fields. Four studies were identified that contained relevant data of sufficient quality that could be used in this risk assessment (see Table 1). Criteria for identifying data quality included the robustness of study methodologies and whether the detected chemicals and their respective concentrations were truly related to artificial turf as
Page 6 of 16
6 opposed to resulting from some other source. For example, some studies measured background sources by placing air samplers in areas upwind of the fields and in various non-turf locations to determine the compounds most likely to be artificial turf related and not due to some other source. Some studies also used laboratory methods to extract all the chemicals from artificial turf infill (i.e., crumb rubber infill) to compare known constituents of turf from those constituents identified in air. This particular risk assessment considered the inhalation route of exposure only, meaning the evaluation considered the potential health impact to players on the field inhaling chemicals in the air that could have come from the field. The 4 studies from which the data were compiled represented various levels of player activity (e.g., heavy activity generates more dust), field age (e.g., older fields volatilize fewer chemicals), and weather conditions (e.g., hot weather causes more chemicals in turf to volatilize in air). Therefore, to be the most conservative, only the maximum levels of detected chemicals were evaluated in the risk assessment, meaning the assessment overestimates potential risk so that a worst-case scenario could be evaluated for potential health impact. By this logic, if a worst case scenario does not result in unacceptable risk, more moderate cases would not either. During the data evaluation and hazard identification step, data was compiled from these 4 field studies and pooled for evaluation (see Table 1). Next, chemicals that were detected over background levels (i.e., chemicals related to the artificial turf) were compared to environmental screening levels to determine which chemicals should be evaluated further for potential risk. Screening levels are set with extreme conservatism, typically with several orders of magnitude buffer to account for any uncertainty in the toxicological studies used to derive them. The screening levels are set by regulatory agencies based on the environmental media evaluated. For this risk assessment, the regional screening levels for residential outdoor air provided by US EPA were used. A residential exposure represents the most conservative scenario, meaning that these levels were set for people who could be exposed every day for 24 hours a day. In reality, players only would be exposed for the length of time they are on the field which would be much less than 24/7. Therefore, this screening process overestimates potential exposures for the purpose of ruling out any chemicals that would not likely cause harm. Only those chemicals above their respective screening levels could potentially contribute to adverse health outcomes (i.e., “drive” risk). However, exceeding a screening level does not mean that adverse risk is present. Exceeding a screening level just flags a compound as requiring further evaluation to determine whether it could contribute to potentially unacceptable levels of risk. The screening step is simply a way to narrow down the focus of a risk evaluation to only those chemicals that, depending on the intensity and level of exposure, could possibly contribute to an adverse health outcome. As seen in Table 1, only 3 chemicals (i.e., 1,2,4-Trimethylbenzene, benzene, and chloroform) were identified above respective screening levels and were evaluated further to determine

Dose-Response Assessment Dose-response data and relevant toxicological parameters for the 3 chemicals identified in step one were compiled from databases maintained by the US EPA and the California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA). In order of priority, the EPA Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) was first checked for toxicity parameters. EPA IRIS typically contains toxicity parameters derived from the literature that have gone through several layers of peer-review and have the most robust support from the toxicology literature. If IRIS values were not available, EPA Provisional Peer Reviewed Toxicity Values (PPRTVs) were used. These values typically cover chemical compounds that have not been as readily studied or have data from emerging literature that has not undergone as extensive a peer-review. Finally, if not PPRTVs were available, the CA OEHHA values were used. See Table 3 for more details. Exposure Assessment The assumptions that go into assessing potential exposure are summarized in Table 2. For this assessment, the assumption was made that children ages 3-15 may be playing on outdoor artificial turf surfaces for 3 hours daily for 180 days out of each year over the course of 12 years. These assumptions are consistent with the assumptions made in a similar assessment by the state of Connecticut and represent conditions of seasonal play (e.g., it is assumed that for half the year the fields could be inaccessible or infrequently used, for example, in winter). The basis for the assumptions is also derived from the EPA Exposure Factors Handbook, which has compiled information on the typical length of sports activities and outdoor play bracketed by age group. See Table 2 for more details. Assumptions can be adjusted as necessary to take into account actual conditions. Because ingestion of the crumb rubber infill by active players on artificial turf fields is likely to be accidental, inhalation is the exposure route of focus in this assessment. For example, players on a field can’t help but inhale air while they are playing – everybody breathes air and people take in more air (breathe at a faster rate) when they are active. Ingestion may be an exposure route of concern for very young children (e.g., toddlers, who are prone to frequent hand to mouth activity) but would not be a primary exposure route of concern for children and adults that use the field for sports and recreational activities. If necessary, a separate assessment may be conducted for this scenario.

8 Risk Characterization The final step in the risk assessment is the characterization of risk of adverse health impact from exposure to chemicals related to artificial turf. This step involves combining the information on exposure and dose-response to come up with a quantitative estimate of risk. The estimated potential risk is expressed differently for compounds that have the potential to cause cancer and those that do not. See Table 4 for the non-cancer risk calculations and Table 5 for the cancer risk calculations. For non-cancer, (Table 4) the hazard quotient is less than 1, meaning in the worst case scenario of exposure, the estimated exposure dose is less than the level below which we would expect adverse health effects. For cancer, (Table 5) the risk predicted is less than 1x10-6, meaning that in a population of similarly exposed people, the likelihood that a person will develop cancer over their lifetime is less than the baseline cancer rate in the population. Therefore, given the scenario evaluated in this limited risk assessment, no unusual or unacceptable non-cancer or cancer risk is predicted in these calculations. 5. Conclusions and Limitations For this risk assessment, the Cambridge Public Health Department estimated the likelihood of adverse health impacts from exposure to chemicals associated with artificial turf fields. There are limitations to the study scope as follows: Only data compiled to date from the literature on chemicals associated with artificial turf fields were used in this assessment. No field data in Cambridge were collected for this study. However, the data is derived primarily from studies that have collected field data in climates similar to Cambridge, including Connecticut and New York. Because only the inhalation pathway was considered in this risk assessment, and because the general nature of volatile organic chemicals (which are associated with artificial turf) is to enter the air more readily at higher temperatures, data from similar climates may be considered representative of conditions in Cambridge. Note that where data were not available for similar climates, data from other studies were used (EPA 2009, CA 2010). The data from the literature are primarily limited to results obtained from a certain type of artificial turf infill, namely SBR (styrene-butadiene rubber). SBR is composed primarily of ground-up recycled tires such that the composition of the infill often contains numerous heavy metals, particularly zinc from the tire belts, volatile and semi-volatile organic chemicals from constituents in the rubber, and PAHs associated with rubber and asphalt/roadways (Zhang et al 2008, EPA 2009, NY 2009, TRC 2009, CT 2010). Hence this risk assessment serves the primary purpose of deciding whether SBR-based products are acceptable replacements for artificial turf fields. It is the understanding of the
Page 9 of 16
9 Cambridge Public Health Department that the existing fields at Danehy Park contain SBR infill. There is simply not enough data in the literature at this time to fully assess the alternative infill products on the market (e.g., TPE, EPDM, various “organic” infills) using a risk assessment paradigm. There are other reasons to consider these alternatives and they will be addressed in a separate literature review. This risk assessment was limited in scope to the inhalation route of exposure and does not consider the ingestion scenario at this time. Children ages 3 – 15 years were used as the population of concern for this assessment, consistent with risk assessments conducted by other jurisdictions (TRC 2009, CT 2010). To identify concerns related to an ingestion scenario, a separate assessment would need to be conducted. However, the literature to date seems to indicate that such an assessment would not likely result in the risk of adverse health effects. This risk assessment is also limited to a worst case scenario evaluation. Such an evaluation overestimates risk of health effects from exposure. Although the conditions above limit the scope of the study, the results of this assessment indicate no unacceptable levels of cancer or non-cancer risk. Because a worst case scenario was evaluated, it is unlikely that children playing on artificial turf fields with characteristics similar to those found in the literature and a scenario similar to that evaluated would result in adverse health effects.
