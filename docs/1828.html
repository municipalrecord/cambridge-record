1) Highlights: • Bicycle riders often park in inappropriate places such as trees or handicap posts • We sampled parking behavior in strategic parking spots around the MIT Campus and then applied nudges to the more heavily frequented Mass Ave area around MIT campus to test whether we could curb bad parking behavior • We found that emotional appealing messages were effective in reducing bicycle parking to trees; whereas more functionally designed nudging messages were not. 2) Abstract: Problematic bicycle parking infringes the law and pertinent regulations, fosters bad behavior and pressures municipal finances due to additional maintenance costs. Additionally bad bicycle parking can cause aesthetic disutility to cities and become a road hazard that impedes the safe passage of pedestrians and the mobility impaired. While signage currently exists that try to dissuade cyclists from parking in such locations (e.g. "No bicycle parking to any disability/handicap sign pole"), our observations have shown these have a limited effect. Our experiment suggests that behavioral sciences influenced "nudging" signs did foster a better parking behavior around the MIT campus by effectively influencing cyclists to not lock their bikes to trees. Observations showed that one "emotional" sign in particular was able to curb bad parking behavior by as much as 50% relative to the samples observed during the control period. This paper shows both to test the power of behavioral nudging for proper bicycle parking as well as to test practical and emotional messages in order to propose a more comprehensive review of their effectiveness. 2

3) Problem statement: a. Background: Over the past decade bicycle riding has gained momentum in reestablishing itself as a key mobility medium for urban environments (Pucher, Buehler, & Seinen, 2011 ). Cycling trips in Cambridge, Massachusetts have tripled between 2002-2012 (Murphy, 2014). City officials and planners in cities around the world have been keen to embrace bicycles as a cheap and environmentally friendly method of transportation that puts less stress in public ways while promoting a healthy lifestyle. Municipal governments have reacted by gradually adopting bicycle friendly measures, such as bike lanes on roads, bike sharing programs and bicycle parking structures. As the number of cyclists increase, so does the need to provide them with the proper infrastructure and services in order to maintain the enthusiasm and support of people for bicycle use. However, with ridership growth also come challenges of improper use of already existing structures (both natural and man made) for bicycle use. There are a number of cases in which the 'free-range' parking of bicycles leads to undesired outcomes (such as parked to: fire hydrants, hand railings, benches, trees, trash receptacles and parking meters or any sign pole, light pole and utility pole that has a sign designating a disability/handicap parking space (Murphy, 2014). The concerns with these can be aesthetic, safety, inconvenience for pedestrians and mobility impaired. This behavior fosters a disregard for the law, while increasing tensions between bicycle and non-bicycle users; furthermore, it puts pressure on municipal finances by forcing additional maintenance expenses for existing structures, posts and trees (Hossain, Botma, Vandebona, & Kiyota, 2003); as such it has to be curbed. 3

If so, how? 4) Method: Experiment design Target population: Cyclists with improper parking behaviors on campus, most of who are MIT students and staff. Quasi-experiment sequence: 1. Locate places where improper bike parking happens and choose strategic site as a sample spot for experiment; 2. Monitor inappropriate parking incidences at high frequency throughout the control group days; 3. Place different nudging signs on different sample spots; 4. Monitor parking behavior at each spot post-sign placement and test the effectiveness of different nudging messages. Sample spots: To determine the experimental sites, we first canvassed the campus to identify the places where improper bike parking happens for a week. During this time, we observed that inappropriate parking behaviors occurred most frequently around building entrances. We then chose three of these sites for a two-day trial observation, including a section of the Massachusetts avenue (the entrance of building 9, building 7 and building 5), the intersection of the Main street and the Ames street (the entrance of building 76) and a section of the Ames street (the entrance of building E14 and E15). 5

Type of Intervention and graph design: We designed two different nudging signs in order to test whether different types of messages yielded different nudging results. The message types are the following: • Functional Sign: This sign is composed of a simple bicycle parking sign and an arrow pointing to the parking lot nearby with the purpose of provide a clear instruction on where people should park. This type of sign was post~d around Parking Meters and Handicap Posts. On posts featuring handicap parking signs, our additional sign complements existing signage stating that bike locking to those signs is illegal. • Emotional Sign: This sign is composed of an image of a crying tree tied with a bike and the text of "Please park in rack! I take damage easily" with the purpose of making an emotional appeal to people who planned to park their bicycle against the tree. This type of sign was posted only around trees. Data collection strategy: We used observation survey and time-series photography as tools to collect 1st hand data. Specifically, we investigated the improper parking behaviors and the usage level of existing parking lots; We recorded the parking behaviors and potential changes between 0900-1700 along with the parking conditions in the bicycle racks, we did a high sampling frequency of several times per day. We recorded inappropriate parking behaviors at each spot on May 1st, May 2nd and May 5th as the control group, our objective for this was to gauge "normal" conditions under which people usually park their bikes. We did so over multiple days in order to minimize sampling bias and obtain a more thorough image of our control group behavior. On May 6th we placed the messages in the order described earlier taking care as to not interrupt any ongoing parking behavior. From May 7th to May 9th we recorded the parking behaviors from bicycle riders in the target spot; this was our experiment phase. Sampling framework: The sampling framework for our experiment required a high frequency of sampling both during the control group days as well as sampling after the nudging signs have been placed. The idea behind this is to contrast the effects of the signs under different conditions. Bear in mind that under our experimental framework, our control 9

group is not in regard to specific parking spots, but in regards of timeframes of observations (pre-messages/post-messages). As such we have a "control period" which occurred during May 1st, 2nd and 5th, and an "experiment period" during May 7th, 8th and 9th. We chose this particular framework, because we decided it made better sense to contrast the effect of the signs under the same parking area and in similar conditions instead of sampling multiple areas that could have different conditions or characteristics, and pose a risk of systematic bias. The overall sampling strategy could be summarized in the following steps: 1. Locate spots where there is a high frequency of parking (both good and bad) behavior. 2. Divide allocation of 'nudging' messages. 3. Observe bicycle parking behavior on the spots over a control period without any intervention or messages. 4. Place the 'nudging' messages 5. Observe bicycle parking behavior on the spots over an "experiment" period, meaning after the messages have been placed. During the control period, we took 18 observations of the parking behavior in the trees, parking meters and handicap signs throughout the selected sampling spot. For this sampling we kept a log that registered both state of parking per spot (see Appendix 4) as well as whether the bicycles parked were new ones or repeat offenders. For every sample observed we also registered photographic evidence as part of the formal research documentation. After we set up the nudging signs, during the "experiment period", we sampled 24 observations on site using the same log format and photographic registration procedure. This data was then used as our primary research data. 10

Statistical Analysis The change in parking to signs and handicap had no clear trend so we did not analyze it further. We did, however, want to see if the reduction in locking to trees was significant. To perform such an analysis we wm model the behavior as a Poisson arrival rate. This requires that arrivals are independent of each other, an untested assumption, particularly since deviant behavior could be 'contagious'. We tested the goodness of fit of this assumption in R using maximum likelihood estimation (David Meyer.' Achim Zeileis, 2013). The null assumption is a fit on the distribution. We then ran a ratio test (M.P. Fay, 2014; M. Fay, 2014) on the deviant parking rates on tree in the pre and post-condition. With the null hypothesis of the two arrival rates being equal. The alternate was one-tailed, and the null was rejected with p-value = 0.007324. Impact and/or implications This research studied the cyclists' parking behavior in the vicinity of the MIT Campus, seeking to identify measures that can nudge cyclists into respecting parking regulations. These results suggest that simple "nudging" messages can be of help in seeking this goal. As stated before, the effects of both "emotional" and "functional" signs were very different during our observations. But at least one of those signs (the emotional sign placed on trees) showed encouraging results in curbing bad parking behavior, suggesting that campus facility managers in particular and planners in general can use this type of message as a friendly and low cost approach to foster proper bicycle parking. What the research ultimately suggests is that we can indeed "nudge" people to better bicycle parking behavior using properly designed signs. Hence, it would be desirable to test other types of signs for their effectiveness in curbing ill behavior as a way of creating a psychologically grounded parking sign language that can easily be deployed in cities. Considering the rapid adoption pace of bicycles as a main mode of transportation, the implications for this strategy as a form of engagements between the city and it's bicycle users can be both quite relevant and useful. b. Limitations The experiment done had several inherent limitations. First and foremost we have to acknowledge that due to the limited sampling size the results can only be described as exploratory. Ideally the experiment would have run for at least a week in both the pre and post conditions, with counts compared for a same weekday-time 17

combination. Statistical analysis shows that the assumption that parking to trees is Poisson-distributed is weak, further exploration would be required to see to what degree is deviance correlated (or contagious). We also didn't control for occupancy in the bike racks. Further experiments are needed in order to validate the findings and make them generalizable. Most important of all, we need to stress that these types of signs are meant to work in conjunction with proper bicycle parking infrastructure, and as such .they are not a substitute for this. Where there is inadequate infrastructure, we would expect to have a higher frequency of undesired bicycle parking behavior. c. Extensions and future research With more time one should test the effectiveness of different types of signage beyond the emotional and functional ones used, testing different signage in different locations around campus. It would also be interested to note the stickiness of behavior change: do the effects of the intervention remain long after the nudging sign age is removed? While a number of studies examine factors influencing cycling as a mode choice using discrete choice models (Akar & Clifton, 2009; Wardman, Tight, & Page, 2007; see Heinen, van Wee, & Maat, 2009 for a full review), some even using the Theory of Planned Behavior to model barriers to cyclo-commuting (Fernandez-Heredia, Monzon, & Jara-Diaz, 2014; Munoz, Monzon, & Lois, 2013), we could not find studies looking at barriers to properly locking one's bike. Beyond obvious real capacity constraints there are other issues such as perceived occupation (a function of rack design), perceptions of safety, rack salience, and distance. Knowing how these parameters affect bike-parking decisions can guide investments of limited space and money for bike racks. This could be done through a combination of a survey to quantify perceptions and observations to quantify choices made with respect to design elements, analyzed using discrete choice models. These 18

Conclusion We began this experiment with a simple research question: Can we nudge people into better bicycle parking behavior? The results from our intervention using emotional signage on trees shows that it is likely possible to effect parking behavior change. However, the results from the informational nudge on parking meters and signs show that both further observation and experimentation on signage types is required.
