Dear Mr. DePasquale: This is provided in response to the above-referenced Awaiting Report. The fifth numbered item in the Council Order asks: "What interventions the City is considering, such as 'Open to Local Residents Only' ...to minimize the disruptive impact of navigational devices on local streets;" in other words, whether the City may restrict access to streets, either generally or at unspecified times, to specific users or groups, such as residents of a street or residents of Cambridge. Without having more details about the restrictions that are being considered, it is difficult to opine upon the legality of a specific regulation that might seek to limit access to City streets Also, a regulation of this nature could potentially conflict with state and/or federal law.' For the reasons discussed below, a municipality is not clearly prohibited from restricting access to public ways; however, such a regulation may be vulnerable to legal challenge. As an initial matter, a municipality has general authority to regulate the use of motor vehicles on public ways. See G. L. c. 40, § 22 (authorizing a municipality "to make ordinances or bylaws for the regulation of carriages and vehicles used therein"); G.L. c. 85, § 2 (authorizing a municipality to regulate traffic in conformance with MassDOT's current manual on uniform traffic control devices and seek MassDOT approval in prescribed situations). While "[t]he power 1 For example, a regulation that has the effect of prohibiting non-local businesses from accessing public ways, while allowing access to Cambridge-based business, may violate the "Dormant Commerce Clause" - a doctrine of constitutional law that courts have inferred from the Commerce Clause in Article I of the United States Constitution. See Opinion of the Justices to the House of Representatives, 428 Mass. 1201, 1207(1998) ("That a measure enacted by a city or other locality discriminates against both out-of-State interests and in- State interests outside that locality does not deflect analysis under the dormant commerce clause.") to regulate the use of public ways which has been delegated to municipalities is not unlimited...every presumption is to be indulged in favor of the validity of such regulations...." Commonwealth v. Dobbins, 344 Mass. 272, 275 (1962) (overnight parking ban was not shown as an unreasonable exercise of the municipal regulatory authority). "Cities have a wide discretion as to what is reasonable in particular situations." Id., citing Commonwealth v. Sargent, 330 Mass. 690, 692 (1953) (street parking prohibition that exempted vehicles owned or used by members and officers of the Massachusetts Legislature was not unreasonable and did not unconstitutionally discriminate or deny equal protection of laws to the public). Despite the general authority of a municipality to regulate the use of public ways, any such proposed regulation could be challenged as potentially being in conflict with state and/or federal law. See American Motorcyclist Association et. al. v. Park Comm. of Brockton, 412 Mass. 753, 756-57 (1992) (regulation banning the use of motorcycles within parkland subject to a park commission's jurisdiction was (i) in conflict with "the privilege of operating motor vehicles on the ways of the commonwealth conferred by a license..." as set forth in G.L. c. 90, § 1; and (i) was not authorized by G.L. c. 90, § 18, which provides "the right to regulate the use and operation of motor vehicles or to ban them entirely...it does not allow the selection of a particular class [of motor vehicles]...for prohibition.") In the present case, while the City is not attempting to ban a specific type of vehicle, the American Motorcyclist Ass'n. case might be interpreted by a court to invalidate the selective exclusion of vehicles from certain public ways. See American Motorcyclist Ass'n 412 Mass. at 756-57. Similar questions of municipal authority have been found to be obstacles to restrictions of this nature in some other jurisdictions; see, e.g. Rumford v. City of Berkeley, 31 Cal.3d 545, 551 (1982) (Supreme Court of California held that state laws did not authorize municipality to close neighborhood streets to through traffic. For the reasons discussed above, while the City would not be expressly prohibited from restricting access to public ways, this type of regulation may be vulnerable to legal challenge. Any specific regulation that would seek to exclude certain vehicles from certain streets (possibly only at certain times) should be further reviewed by this office prior to its promulgation. If any such regulation were proposed, we would work with the City Manager and the Director of Traffic, Parking and Transportation to review it prior to its promulgation.
