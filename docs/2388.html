Attachment G Cambridge Inclusionary Housing Study: DEVELOPER SUGGESTIONS, COMMENTS, AND QUESTIONS July 7, 2016 CDD staff have met with members of the development and business community to discuss and seek feedback on the Inclusionary Housing Study recently completed by David Paul Rosen and Associates (DRA). When the study was released, CDD forwarded it to 30 developers and offered to be available to anyone who wanted to discuss the study and its recommendations. CDD staff met with developers both individually and as part of larger groups organized through the Cambridge Chamber of Commerce and Kendall Square Association. Developers and groups CDD staff met with include: Alexandria Real Estate, Biomed Realty, Boston Properties, Criterion Development, Hanover, HYM, Forest City, MITIMCO, and Twining Properties, and the Cambridge Redevelopment Authority. DRA's principal also met with the development committee of the Kendall Square Association (KSA) which also submitted written comments and questions. In addition, the Chamber of Commerce is currently undertaking a peer review of DRA's report. The following summarizes the suggestions, comments, and questions that were raised by in these meetings. SUGGESTIONS RAISED BY DEVELOPERS • Offsets Developers noted that the existing inclusionary program has been a great success and has produced a lot of affordable units. Changes to the program may result in the program no longer working. Incentives are needed to offset the costs associated with increasing the set- aside ratio. Developers raised ideas which might help offset the impact of an increased inclusionary set-aside standard on the feasibility of residential projects. These include: • Relaxed dimensional standards - access to additional height, relaxed setbacks and other changes to better allow developments to access the inclusionary density bonus; • reducing parking requirements and/or allowing above-grade parking; • more flexibility or streamlining with permitting and/or expedited permitting for residential developments, especially for smaller developments; tax incentives in the form of reduced real estate taxes for developments with inclusionary units; • changes to TDR requirements and allowances in Kendall; • vary requirements for different size developments to reflect unique financial models for each type. 1

• Timing/Effective Date Developers noted that the timing of the applicability of new requirements could impact the success of developments already in progress, including projects in early phases for which where land costs have been fixed. Some noted that they had negotiated land prices and underwritten development costs based on the current inclusionary requirements. Many recommended phasing in the increased standards, starting with the true 15% and/or establishing a date in the future when the changes would become effective to avoid disrupting projects now in process. The effective date suggested varied but was as late as January 1, 2018. • Periodic Review of Inclusionary Ordinance Developers agreed with DRA and City staff that it makes sense to conduct a periodic review of the ordinance. It was suggested that the ordinance should be reviewed on a regular basis, every 5 years or so recognizing that it may take 5 years to assess the impact of any changes. • Affordable Unit Locations Developers noted that the existing ordinance is very clear, so they know what is expected. Most developers said that it is important to retain the clarity and predictability of the inclusionary requirements so it can guide development decisions, however several suggested that more flexibility in where affordable units are located in a building would be beneficial. GENERAL COMMENTS • Process Developers are concerned that the process is moving very quickly and warrants more careful consideration given the significance of the proposed changes. • Study Assumptions/Analysis Concerns were raised regarding the assumptions used in the financial modelling section of study. Specifically, developers noted that some development costs had increased since the study was completed; land values in the model were low; and that capital costs will increase. They inquired about the source of the assumptions and whether construction costs in the models reflected union or non-union labor. • Impact of Set-aside Increase on Residential Housing Production Developers are concerned about the feasibility of development with an increase in the inclusionary set aside from 11.5% to 20%. They noted that putting a project together is complex and that it could be challenging to make the numbers work. It was noted that while 2

80/20 projects (80% market/20% affordable), do work in other communities, they are typically seen in communities (mostly suburbs) where it is less expensive to build. They view the proposed set-aside increase as burdensome and that it may result in unintended consequences. It could impact housing production and result in fewer affordable units if housing production slows, thus not creating more affordable units nor more family sized units. • Decreased Land Value and Impact on Commercial/Residential Balance It was noted that the proposed changes could result in a decrease in residential land values. Some developers said that if residential land values are reduced, owners may opt to hold land rather than develop it as housing, and/or they might seek to develop office or lab instead of residential. • Reviewing IZ within context of other City requirements Developers noted that an increase in inclusionary set-asides should be evaluated in the context of other requirements placed on new development, including public realm and infrastructure requirements, mitigation payments, incentive zoning contributions. Several noted that any increase in the percentage of inclusionary units should not be considered in isolation, but with other requirements and expectations of the City which add to the overall cost of development. • Exchange of Premium Units for Increased IZ Units/Unit Location Some developers are open to the idea of more flexibility in the location of units in return for larger, but not more, affordable units. In general, there is a preference for more flexibility on distribution of the units, with an understanding that the units need to be spread throughout the building and that only the most valuable units should not be designated as affordable to allow for higher value units to help offset the costs of affordable units. • Unit Size and Distribution Developers were generally open to the idea of basing the inclusionary requirements on square footage in order to provide larger units, with some preferring this approach. It was also noted that there could be design challenges in providing larger units and these could impact unit locations within the building. There is a concern that the proposed changes will actually not further the City's goal of producing more family sized units. • Density Bonus Developers said they have had difficulty accessing the full amount of the density bonus as they are often restricted by dimensional requirements, such as height, setbacks, and other restrictions. In some cases, they are able to take advantage of increased FAR, but not unit count. Occasionally, project size sometimes has to be reduced due to community concerns, 3

Some developers stated that the density bonus should be increased to reflect the cost of creating the affordable units. • Income Eligibility and Targeting Developers were receptive to the idea of including a middle-income component since it would be expected to produce more rental income than lower income housing. However, it was noted that a higher-income set-aside, particularly 100% to 120% AMI, is less advantageous if those units are slower to rent due to less demand for middle-income rental units, as has been seen in recent cases. • Ongoing Involvement in Process Developers expressed an interest in staying involved in the process, many noting that they want to be helpful. They said that they want to be certain that their concerns are heard and considered, and they have an interest in the details of the petition as it is drafted. With regard to the last point, some emphasized that it was critical since the recommendations will be further developed in the petition, and the content will have a significant impact on the economics of developments. • Utility Allowances There was a concern that utility allowances for tenants in affordable units were too high and did not account for utility costs in new sustainably-designed buildings that are lower than other building types. QUESTIONS • Developers wondered if, how, and when the market would adjust to the recommended change and whether the change would lead to some combination of increased market rents, lowered housing production, and lowered land values. • What has the experience been in other places in terms of reaching an equilibrium between commercial and residential when requirements are increased? Has it worked? Would it be advisable to ask an economist should look at the report in light of the local regulatory environment and costs associated with other public benefits? • At what stage in the permitting process does a development need to be in order to go forward under the current inclusionary provisions - issuance of a Special Permit or Building Permit? What about minor or major amendments to Special Permits? Developers are concerned about development projects now being advanced, especially those in PUD districts since these 4
