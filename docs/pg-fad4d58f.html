1 MINUTES OF THE CAMBRIDGE CITY COUNCIL PUBLIC SAFETY COMMITTEE Wednesday, April 29, 2026 PUBLIC SAFETY COMMITTEE MEMBERS Councillor Al-Zubi, Chair Councillor McGovern Councillor Nolan Councillor Simmons Councillor Sobrinho-Wheeler A public meeting of the Cambridge City Council’s Public Safety Committee was held on Wednesday, April 29, 2026. The meeting was Called to Order at 3:00 p.m. by the Chair, Councillor Al-Zubi Pursuant to Chapter 2 of the Acts of 2025 adopted by Massachusetts General Court and approved by the Governor, the City is authorized to use remote participation. This public meeting was hybrid, allowing participation in person, in the Sullivan Chamber, 2nd Floor, City Hall, 795 Massachusetts Avenue, Cambridge, MA and by remote participation via Zoom. At the request of the Chair, Clerk of Committees Erwin called the roll. Councillor Al-Zubi – Present/In Sullivan Chamber Councillor McGovern – Present/In Sullivan Chamber Councillor Nolan – Present/In Sullivan Chamber Councillor Simmons – Present/Remote Councillor Sobrinho-Wheeler – Present/In Sullivan Chamber Present – 5. Chair Al-Zubi offered opening remarks and noted that the Call of the meeting was to review the Cambridge Police Department’s use of ShotSpotter technology, also known as SoundThinking, which was referred to the Public Safety Committee through the Annual Surveillance Report, CMA 2026-44, on March 9. 2026. Chair Al-Zubi recognized Stephanie Guirand who gave a presentation titled “Community Sentiment on ShotSpotter”. The presentation was provided in advance of the meeting and included in the Agenda Packet. Chair Al-Zubi recognized Mason Kortz who offered comments related to efforts obtaining information about ShotSpotter through public records requests, adding that it appears Cambridge does not have a direct contract with SoundThinking. Mason Kortz shared concerns about transparency, challenges obtaining records related to contracts, data access, and the overall performance of the technology. Mason Kortz noted that Courts have also raised concerns about who owns the data collected and whether it can be shared with federal agencies. Mason Kortz encouraged the City to be more transparent with the public in order to fully be able to evaluate how effective ShotSpotter is in the Cambridge community. Chair Al-Zubi recognized Spencer Piston who gave a presentation titled “Can SoundThinking data be shared with ICE?”. The presentation was provided in advance of the meeting and included in the Agenda Packet. Chair Al-Zubi recognized Gideon Epstein who pointed out that ShotSpotter does not meet the standards that are required through the Surveillance Technology Ordinance and emphasized that the City Council needs to review whether the benefits of ShotSpotter outweigh the costs related to civil rights and civil liberties. Gideon Epstein noted that Cambridge does not have control over data sharing, retention, and access, which creates even more concern about the lack of privacy. Gideon Epstein spoke in support of the City Council discontinuing the use of ShotSpotter. Chair Al-Zubi recognized Acting Commissioner Wells who gave a presentation titled “Cambridge Police ShotSpotter Program”. The presentation was provided in advance of the meeting and included in the Agenda Packet. During the presentation the Chair made a motion to extend the meeting. Chair Al-Zubi recognized Councillor Sobrinho-Wheeler who thanked the presenters and shared that he was interested in the community survey because it provided direct feedback from residents about ShotSpotter. Councillor Sobrinho-Wheeler added that residents do request public safety resources, but very rarely has he heard anyone request ShotSpotter or surveillance technology. Councillor Sobrinho-Wheeler asked for more information on how the survey was conducted. Stephanie Guirand explained that the survey was created with help from a Tufts University Professor and members of the Coalition who canvassed the community and held educational events where residents were invited to complete the voluntary survey which is why it was a smaller number Stephanie noted that the survey does not generalize findings. Those who participated shared concerns about profiling and placements of ShotSpotter throughout the City. Councillor Sobrinho-Wheeler emphasized his concerns about the accuracy of ShotSpotter and pointed out that it does miss some gunshots and has false alerts, which can make it unreliable for public safety purposes. Councillor Sobrinho-Wheeler shared that he remains opposed to the use of ShotSpotter in Cambridge. Chair Al-Zubi recognized Councillor McGovern who thanked the presenters and community members present at the meeting and pointed out that the Council has discussed ShotSpotter many times over the years. Councillor McGovern acknowledged both sides of the arguments made by supporters and those opposed, and shared concerns about privacy and how the information is shared. Councillor McGovern asked for more information related to when ShotSpotter contributed to a positive outcome. Acting Commissioner Wells provided an example of a Cambridge case and explained how ShotSpotter was an effective tool in the investigation. Spencer Piston shared that he has not seen evidence that ShotSpotter has improved police response times in Cambridge but acknowledged it is possible it may help officers respond faster in some cases. Spencer Piston pointed out that if ShotSpotter was effective, there would be stronger evidence that shows it is directly successful to investigations or convictions. Councillor McGovern had clarifying questions related to the funding of ShotSpotter and if that money could go towards something else if the city no longer used ShotSpotter. Megan Bayer explained that it is funded through a regional grant through the Boston Area Security Initiative and the funds may be able to be used for a different security initiative. Councillor McGovern asked if the city could stop using ShotSpotter if it is through a regional program and who can make the decision to stop using it. Megan Bayer shared that the City Manager determines what public safety tools are used, but the City Council also has authority under the Surveillance Technology Ordinance. Megan Bayer added that the Council can approve or withdraw the use of the technology. Councillor McGovern emphasized that the topic of ShotSpotter has been discussed many times and that the City Council should decide on how they want to move forward. Chair Al-Zubi recognized Councillor Nolan who asked for clarification regarding the City Council’s role in approving ShotSpotter under the Surveillance Ordinance. Councillor Nolan questioned if the positive outcomes from the technology would have happened regardless through reports from the community and police investigations and asked if ShotSpotter is always recording. Megan Bayer provided a brief overview of how ShotSpotter was first implemented after the City Council shared interest in the technology, and provided information on two ways the City Council can move forward with stopping the use of it.

5 Chair Al-Zubi made a motion to extend the meeting by ten minutes. Clerk of Committees Erwin called the roll. Councillor Al-Zubi – Yes Councillor McGovern – Yes Councillor Nolan – Yes Councillor Simmons – Yes Councillor Sobrinho-Wheeler -Yes Yes 5. Motion passed. Chair Al-Zubi recognized Deputy Superintendent Boyle to respond to Councillor Nolan’s questions and comments. Deputy Superintendent Boyle explained that ShotSpotter is always operating, but only sends alerts when it detects possible gunfire, adding that police receive one second of audio before and after the detected gunshot and that data is overridden after 30 hours unless it is needed for investigation. Spencer Piston shared that he did not agree with Deputy Superintendent Boyle and that the microphones are listening and recording, noting that records have shown that conversations before a detection have been recorded. Acting Commissioner Wells shared that audio is only retained temporarily for investigation purposes and that conversations do not meet the sound threshold needed for ShotSpotter alerts. Councillor Nolan agreed that the City Council needs to move forward with a decision on whether they want to continue using this technology. Chair Al-Zubi emphasized that Cambridge is a sanctuary city and asked about the privacy risks associated with ShotSpotter, questioning if using this technology aligns with the City’s commitment to protecting residents. Mason Kortz pointed out that Cambridge does not have a direct contract with SoundThinking and that the contract language gives the company control over the data. Mason Kortz shared concerns about resident information being shared without their knowledge and agreed that the technology does not align with the City’s commitment to protecting residents. Acting Commissioner Wells pointed out that if voices are recorded, there is no way to identify who is speaking or where the voice came from. Deputy Superintendent Boyle added that data cannot be shared with other agencies unless there is a formal agreement signed by both parties which is also approved through SoundThinking. Megan Bayer shared that the current contract gives SoundThinking ownership of the data and allows participating agencies to use it for law enforcement purposes and would notify the Police Department if it received a subpoena for data. Chair Al-Zubi shared concerns about who receives the data and who can gain access to it and questioned if the benefits of the technology outweigh the privacy and civil rights concerns. Chair Al-Zubi offered closing remarks and thanked the participants for their input and noted that the City Council would continue reviewing the use of the technology. Chair Al-Zubi made a motion to adjourn the meeting. Clerk of Committees Erwin called the roll. Councillor Al-Zubi – Yes Councillor McGovern – Yes Councillor Nolan – Yes Councillor Simmons – Yes
